I've Basically Bought Out All the Colima Saddles

August 31, 2018

Before I get to the main story.

I really liked the CC-RD100 (wired, no cadence), the first model in CatEye's "Strada" series,
but the click-tech mechanism adopted after this model—where the buttons are on the underside instead of the top and the device itself moves to click them—has a problem.
With regular use, the body gets wobbly relative to the mount.
With my usage frequency, it doesn't last two years.
To put it another way, about three battery lifespans.
Even if you replace the mount-side parts, the grooves that are actually worn are on the device body itself,
so it doesn't solve the problem. And this issue remains unresolved to this day.

The CC-RD200, the wired version with cadence of this same line, is still going strong for some reason,
but the CC-RD300W (wireless, no cadence) and CC-RD400DW (dual wireless, with cadence) are discontinued.
The 300 and 400 are models from around 2008.
This is just speculation, but when a high-brightness LED front light and wireless cycle computer are positioned too close,
the cycle computer's reception gets blocked and speed information and such disappear.
Maybe the 300 and 400's radio method was especially prone to this, so they discontinued them quickly.
The fact that the CC-RD200—the wired model that came before them—is still around seems to support this theory.
If CatEye only made lights or only made cycle computers, they could've brushed it off,
but their own products were fighting each other, which was probably bad for business.

I liked the RD100 because it worked fine even when used alongside heart rate monitors, which were unstable on almost every brand back then.
By the time the RD100 was discontinued, simple single-function wired cycle computers from other makers too were becoming an endangered species.
That's when I happened to notice the cycle computer in the image above.
It's literally a CC-RD100 model, but it came out as a Pinarello accessory,
and even years after the RD100 was discontinued, you could still get it
(though it's impossible to find now).


I got in some Colima saddles.
Looking at the catalog images, the base model is Selle Italia's Flite,
with Colima's logo embroidered on it.
I'd been wondering about it for a while, and I thought,
"Wait, could this actually be a Flite?" So I decided to order some.

I know this sounds weird, but the Flite in the line above refers to the Flite in the broad sense, and the Flite refers to the narrow sense.
Since I wasn't sure if it was truly a Flite, I started cautiously with just one.
Once I confirmed it was a Flite, I went bold and ordered plenty more.




Now, shifting gears—I'm a collector of Selle Italia Turbos,
and I said way back early in this blog that I'd write about it eventually,
but I still haven't.
I have regular Turbos, striped Bie Turbos, squishy gel Turbo Gels,
Turbo Specials with metal like San Marco Rolls on the front and rear,
Turbo Triathlons with a center groove, Lady Turbos with a wider rear,
Lady Turbo Specials (Turbo Special construction but wider rear),
Turbo Juniors for younger riders with short front and rear, aluminum-rail Turbo SLG (Super Leggera),
manganese-rail Pro Team Turbos, and so on.

The Turbo has a reissue model called "Turbo 1980," and the one in the image above is my personal one with brown nubuck that's aged nicely.
The Turbo 1980 is so expertly reissued that even I—a Turbo expert (not an official certification)—have a hard time judging from the side view whether it's a reissue or not.
Well, the vast majority of Turbos you see these days are Turbo 1980s unless they're clearly on a vintage bike.

The Flite saddle has gone through major shape changes that make it a different product, which counts as three generations now.
The second generation has a hole in the nose with a plastic "Flite" nameplate insert.
I personally think that if a saddle changes this drastically, the brand shouldn't keep the same model name.
I've never tried the third generation, but when I used the second-gen Flite,
I thought, "This isn't a Flite at all."
That's not to say the second-gen Flite is a bad saddle.
I just wanted it to carry forward most of what the original Flite represented.
Among Selle Italia saddles, maybe only the SLR maintains that philosophy without bending too much.
The second-gen Flite is discontinued, but the original Flite, like the Turbo, has a reissue called Flite 1990.

But unlike Turbo 1980, Flite 1990 is hard to call a worthy recreation of the original—it's kind of awkward.
So I decided to write an article about it and asked friend A,
"You had a bike with a Flite 1990 on it, right? Bring it by,"
and he came right over. But...


Hey! This isn't a Flite 1990—this is an original Flite!
That's no good for an article! (Though it is, actually.)
Friend A definitely has a Flite 1990 too.
Since the original Flite became unavailable,
I remember him reluctantly buying it as a substitute, thinking it might not be adequate.


So,

I ordered some for the article.

Can you spot the difference?


↑This is a Colima Flite.
The Flite was groundbreakingly thin for its time,
and switching to one from a traditional saddle let you get 2–3 cm more seatpost setback.
Back then, only horizontal frames existed, so lots of people cared about how minimal seatpost exposure looked.
To maximize seatpost extension, you need not just a thin base but also
a shape that doesn't "lift" the rails.
The original Flite has the base and rails very close together,
thinner than most saddles you see today.
The cushioning (padding) on the base is thick, so it's slightly thicker than a carbon-fiber-like spatula saddle, but still.


With Flite 1990, they've switched to raised rails.
I understand this was unavoidable.
The original Flite's base and rails are so close
that if the seatpost clamp assembly's parts above the rails are thick,
they interfere right away.
They probably switched to raised rails to avoid that,
but, well, calling it an "original Flite reissue" feels like a stretch.

To be clear, Flite 1990 isn't a bad saddle.
Some people have found Flite 1990 to be the most comfortable saddle they've ever used.
That's separate from the point that treating Flite 1990 as identical to the original Flite is unreasonable.

Anyway, Selle Italia saddles can have their manufacturing date pinned down.


This is that Inoue Turbo Junior from earlier.
The number in the center is the manufacturing year, and the letters around it represent the month.
It has nothing particularly to do with the Sanctuary hourglasses from Saint Seiya.
In the order G·F·M·A·M·G·L·A·S·O·N·D, these are the first letters of the months 1–12 in Italian,
but since months 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12 match English month initials,
you can figure it out pretty easily.
A missing dot for a month means either the manufacturing month
or—if dots are missing starting from G through, say, A—it's April,
but for some reason this 1986 model is only missing August and December dots.
That's pretty sloppy!
Maybe in 1986 they concentrated Turbo production only in August and December.
By the way, pre-1985 models sometimes lack this date mark entirely.


January, June, and July start with G because Italian has no J.
It's used in foreign words like Jeep, judo, and jazz, but.
So JoJo's Bizarre Adventure Part 5, set in Italy, is GIOGIOS, not JOJO.
Though honestly, nobody calls Giorno "JoJo" anyway,
and that's not unique to Part 5, so let's not worry about it.

The date marking system on current models has changed to
"an arrow pointing across the year indicates the manufacturing month,"
but

this Colima Flite has both the dot system and arrow marked together.
It looks like November 2001, but in any case, 2001 is certain.


Friend A's black Flite was May 1996.
Why the 2001 model lacks the dot marks that the 1996 model has is unclear.


The Flite 1990 in the article is September 2017.
Also, the months are now in Arabic numerals.

Close, but not quite—the nut above the clamp is actually touching the saddle base.
This is why the original Flite is picky about seatposts, and why
Flite 1990 got nervous and switched to raised rails.
Even in close situations, flex causes contact.


Also, the back side of the "Titanium" marking contacts the clamp, and
this area of leather alone gets a squared-off indentation mark from habit—
a common sight on original Flites.

Installing it...


I went with a USE Alien, aluminum red anodized, 25mm diameter seatpost
(there's also an Alien Carbon, but it doesn't come in 25mm).
This is a late-model Alien, but the early model had two parallel M3 long bolts to clamp it—ridiculous—
and the late model has one M4 long bolt to clamp it—also ridiculous.
I might switch to a Thomson Elite eventually.


The saddle and seatpost I used before were a Selle Italia Signo and Thomson Elite.
The Signo—measured at the most forward graspable part of the saddle rails
with calipers from above and below—
is less than 40mm thick. Pretty thin.
The thinnest are Selle Italia's original SLR titanium rails and Specialized's original Toup, at around 35mm.


The Selle Italia Signo and Thorak have an AVS (Axial Pivot System)—
where two rails are first combined and then connected to the base via elastomer—structure.
They flex side-to-side even in your grip, and you feel it in actual comfort too.
The Thorak has carbon rail models, but those don't use AVS.

With AVS, saddle bags can be problematic, and when
securing tubular tires with toe straps, the tire slides forward,
causing slight contact with your hamstring during pedaling.
You can avoid it by not strapping a spare tire to the saddle.


Thomson Elite seatposts have thick front and rear walls,
which was a company patent. Kalloy made a complete knockoff
(I called it "Thomson's Tooth-Wah" to mean "not a Thomson Seatpillar"),
but it disappeared from the market at a speed that can only be explained politically.
Something happened.
Recently I installed a PRO aluminum seatpost with similarly thick front and rear walls—not as extreme,
but—maybe the patent expired, or maybe Kalloy's knockoff violated the clamp design, not the wall thickness.

Thomson Elite normally doesn't have this much wall thickness difference.
This 25.0mm, the thinnest in the lineup, is probably why it's like this.


I did a night ride on the Colima Flite,
and I can confirm—this is the real deal. Hehehehe. ← Stop treating Flite 1990 like a knockoff
Looking back at earlier photos, I basically always set saddles dead level.
Occasionally I'll go up to 1° nose-up.
Of course, for saddles designed to be tilted nose-up like old Selle San Marco Concor or Strada models,
I'll follow the maker's intent, but
those "practical front-riding saddles" aren't for me.

If I set the saddle level on the fitting bench and match height and reach,
I get nearly 100% repeatability.
I'm not dismissing the idea of trying slight nose-down tilts for potential improvement,
but if I commit to only level setup,
any saddle I've used before is instantly usable with the same feel.

The original Flite—haven't used it in like 10 years—brought back the feeling of
that rear-seat position and bottom grip on the drops locking in perfectly.
The saddle nose clearance with my thigh is exactly as I remember.
It's the same as instantly recalling the best brake lever position and grip style
when using a handlebar you've ridden before.
So it's not that the original Flite clicks for everyone—
if you know the original Flite, your muscle memory snaps back instantly.

You might say the same applies to Flite 1990, but
the padding feel on the Colima saddle versus Flite 1990 is totally different.
The rebound when you press your thumb on the rear—the tactile sensation—is completely different.
Original Flites do vary in padding across years, but
at least this Colima saddle falls within the realm of evoking the original Flite.
In other words, this IS an original Flite.

For those who understand that, or for original Flite refugees,
I've basically bought out all the Colima Flites—that's the point of this post.
I left just one with the distributor.
So they can't say "our store bought them all out," and
because stock hasn't moved in at least two years, a sudden disappearance would prove this article's impact. It's an experiment.
And I'm confident we'll sell through our current inventory. Hehehehe.

this might be the last chance to get one.

I got permission to photograph the real Colima carbon saddle

Frame discretion wasn't prioritized in the cropping.


Yellow elastomer is adhered to the saddle rails,
reducing clamp damage.
The saddle base edge is sharp, so
it's carefully covered and glued.


The two yellow dots at the saddle rear are elastomer
cushioning the saddle rails.

giving it a ride feel you wouldn't expect from a no-padding saddle.
