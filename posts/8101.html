Rebuilt the Dahon rear wheel

November 10, 2026

Here we go with wheels again (and so on).

↑A rear wheel I built nearly 20 years ago with a ZIPP MID-V280 rim, and it's mine.


Tni old wing hub
(10-speed freewheel body that cannot be converted to 11-speed)
24H all CX-RAY nylon 2-cross lacing, with the 2-cross (1 cross) on the freewheel side being all inpokes.

With 2-cross lacing, the flanges have significant thickness, so in the normal range you can't build a wheel unless you use all inpokes or all outpokes straight through. With large flanges and around 16H, you might be able to mix inpokes and outpokes in a 2-cross pattern, but even then, on the Rolf Prima rear wheel I use 16H with all outpokes on the freewheel side and all inpokes on the non-freewheel side in a 1-cross, deliberately avoiding weaving inpokes and outpokes together. The freewheel side is all outpokes to avoid contact with the rear derailleur when downshifting.

On the rear wheel in the image above, the freewheel side uses all inpokes, but with up to about 10-speed, aero spokes won't contact the rear derailleur. When using Campagnolo components, these conditions get stricter, but even then 10-speed is fine.

Here we go with wheels again (and so on).

Continuing from the other day. I'm rebuilding a 14-inch rear wheel for a Dahon.


It's an external 3-speed, with a top 9T and 4-4T jumps: 9-13-17T. The 9T and freewheel body are Dahon's own, while the 13T and 17T cogs are scavenged Shimano units.


When it came in, there was one spoke missing, but


Looking at the rim holes, the spoke next to it had snapped right at the neck.


Dahon 3-speed freewheel hub, 20H
CN spokes, black #14 plain, 2-2 lacing (radial on both sides) with all inpokes throughout.

This wheel has an issue with the lacing design itself:


↑Freewheel side


↑Non-freewheel side
The spoke on the inside of the final crossing is being pinched by the spoke on the outside, so the spoke neck isn't sitting properly. Apparently, spokes have snapped three times in the past, but this lacing design itself is essentially a ticking time bomb for spoke breakage.

The spokes being used are CN spokes, which are rather difficult to obtain, so it doesn't seem like this wheel was ever rebuilt from its original factory state.


I found where repairs were made. The spoke head on the left side of the image has the original Cn stamp that came with the wheel, but


↑This lowercase 'n' should also be a CN spoke. So this must be a replacement spoke ordered from Dahon as a service part. A shop with a spoke cutter would likely source a DT black Champion or Sapim black Leader for repairs (that being the better approach to avoid future spoke breakage).




Also, this wheel generally has spokes that are too short. The topmost image shows the repair spot because the nipples aren't dirty there, but you can see the different thread exposure—that's because the nipple lengths differ. With a 14mm nipple showing exposed threads in a finished build, the spoke length is definitely too short.

This rear wheel has problems with both its official lacing design and spoke length.


I released the tension, cut the spokes, and disassembled the wheel.


I kept one original spoke from each side.

For this hub and rim, I calculated the spoke length needed for 2-2 lacing on both sides beforehand without preconceptions, then took actual measurements. The measurements came out about 2mm shorter than my calculations on both sides.

This hub has almost no dish. The original spokes differed by 2mm between sides, which mainly stems from the hub being somewhat high-low flange.


I wondered if using all inpokes with 4-cross instead of 2-cross would let the spoke heads sit properly, so I tried running just two spokes.

But it doesn't work. The spokes in this image are #15, but that's beside the point— with all inpokes, the adjacent 4-cross creates extreme overlap again, so the pinched spoke heads lift out of the flange holes.

If this hub were 24H, I could alternate all-inpoke 4-cross with all-outpoke 4-cross to avoid extreme overlap, but that only works for 24H because there's an even number of crossings per flange (6). With 20H (with an odd number of final crossings per side—5), that doesn't work either.

Honestly, I even thought it might be healthier to just radial-lace both sides with Sapim Spokes #13 neck strength, at least that would be better than the original wheel.


Built it.


20H all #14 Champion 4-4 JIS lacing without weaving the final crossing. Since I'm not weaving it, I'm not doing any soldering either (never intended to).


Only the spokes immediately adjacent to the valve hole have turquoise nipples.

Why did I go with JIS lacing on a rim-brake rear wheel...?


Going back in time, this is during the temporary assembly of the wheel. Usually I thread spokes into all flange holes beforehand, but this time I assembled one side at a time. After threading the freewheel-side spokes, I set one non-freewheel side spoke in a right-drop orientation—that's the image above. Since I'm doing symmetrical 2-cross (technically, treating it as +standard rim), right-drop is JIS lacing. In this case, if that outpoke were ever a replacement spoke in a finished wheel, it would pass through the gap in the bundle of final crossings on the opposite flange.


If I had done left-drop instead, the outpoke would end up inside the final crossing, making future maintenance harder.


Without spoke tension, there's flex in the spokes so you can thread them diagonally even with left-drop, but in a finished wheel under tension, you'd have to release tension on the crossing spokes to thread a spoke through, which is annoying. So I went with JIS lacing. Well, going forward, given the material differences in spokes and how well the spoke heads sit now, I don't think we'll see spoke breakage at anywhere near the previous rate.


↑On a side note, I attempted weaving the final crossing only on the non-freewheel side. The image shows the left final crossing temporarily woven. In the end, I decided not to weave on either side.


↑Freewheel side, outer flange


↑Non-freewheel side, outer flange
The hub flange bore deep marks from pinched inpokes, so on both sides I've routed the inpokes to avoid those marks.


↑Freewheel side, outer


↑Freewheel side, inner


↑Non-freewheel side, outer

By using 4-4 lacing instead of all-inpoke or all-outpoke patterns, I no longer need to use all inpokes throughout or all outpokes throughout, so the poor spoke-head seating to the hub flange—the main cause of spoke breaking in the original wheel—has been eliminated.
